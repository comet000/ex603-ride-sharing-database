# Unit 4 Finding: The Real No-Show Rate and Where Leadership Should Look Next

**To:** Leadership team  
**From:** Trust and Data team

**Bottom line:** The platform's actual recorded rider no-show rate is **6.0%, or 12 out of 200 trips**, not the roughly half shown on the old dashboard. We recommend examining the trips associated with **drivers 2 and 3** first. **Driver 4** also has a high percentage, but it comes from only one no-show in six trips, so we'd monitor rather than draw conclusions. A rider no-show is *associated with* a driver in our records; it does **not** establish that the driver caused it.

## What the corrected number includes

The 200 trip records contain **156 completed trips**, **34 cancellations**, and **10 disputes**. Only **12** meet the failure definition carried forward from our Unit 3 audit: a cancellation marked **"Rider no show."** Counting all cancellations or disputes as this type of failure would misrepresent what we're investigating.

There are **15 registered drivers**, but **13 have recorded trips**. Among those 13, **four have no recorded rider no-shows**, **seven have one**, **driver 3 has two**, and **driver 2 has three**. No-shows appear across nine drivers, but the following three deserve closer attention:

| Driver | No-show trips | All trips | Recorded rate | Recommendation |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| **2** | **3** | **16** | **18.75%** | Review first |
| **3** | **2** | **13** | **15.38%** | Review next |
| **4** | **1** | **6** | **16.67%** | Monitor; very small history |

Together, these three drivers were associated with just **35 of the platform's 200 trips (17.5%)**, yet those trips accounted for **half of all recorded rider no-shows (six of 12)**. This makes their records worth examining, but the percentages need to be interpreted alongside the number of trips each driver handled.

To avoid placing too much weight on drivers with very few trips, we chose the **25th percentile of driver trip counts** as our cutoff for comparing individual rates. We used a percentile rather than a fixed number for three reasons: it **recalculates as trip volumes change**; stakeholders can **adjust the cutoff** if operational experience suggests a different standard for sufficient volume; and selecting drivers based on trip counts, rather than choosing a cutoff to fit the observed no-show rates, **helps avoid bias**.

For this dataset, the cutoff is **13 trips**, which 10 of the 13 drivers with recorded trips meet. **Drivers 2 and 3 qualify**, with 16 and 13 trips respectively, while **driver 4 falls below it**, with six trips. The cutoff can change as the data changes. It reduces the influence of the smallest trip histories, but it doesn't guarantee that the remaining drivers' rates represent reliable long-term patterns.

## Where no-shows were recorded

The **North** recorded **six no-shows in 77 trips (7.79%)**, **Central** recorded **five in 83 (6.02%)**, and the **South** recorded **one in 40 (2.50%)**. The whole-platform figure of **6.00%** gives leadership a common reference point for comparing those areas. North is where we'd begin a region-level review, but a few events can shift any of these percentages significantly.

City detail shows why regional totals aren't enough:

| Recorded pickup city | No-show trips | All trips | Recorded rate |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Hue | 4 | 40 | 10.00% |
| Hanoi | 3 | 38 | 7.89% |
| Haiphong | 3 | 39 | 7.69% |
| Saigon | 1 | 40 | 2.50% |
| Danang | 1 | 43 | 2.33% |

**Hue and Danang are both included in Central**, yet Hue has four recorded no-shows and Danang one. The regional average hides that difference. It doesn't mean Hue has a proven ongoing problem; it tells the regional team which trips may deserve closer attention.

We also need to be clear about **whose region** we're discussing. These figures use **where the trip was supposed to start**, not where the driver lives. Only **67 of 200 trips (33.5%)** began in a driver's home region, and just **44 (22.0%)** began in their home city. A director responsible for service in a location may need the pickup view, while a leader managing drivers based in a location may need the home-region view.

**Driver 2** provides a useful example. They're based in **Danang**, but their three recorded no-shows happened during pickups in **Hue (one)** and **Haiphong (two)**. Driver 2 is also the only Danang-based driver with recorded trips, so the home-city results for Danang reflect just one driver's history. That doesn't tell us whether Danang itself has a service problem.

For leadership, the distinction matters. Investigating driver 2 may require coordination between the team responsible for the driver and the teams responsible for pickup operations in Hue and Haiphong. **Home-location summaries help identify which drivers a management team oversees, while pickup-location summaries show where the incidents happened.** Both are useful, but they answer different questions.

## Why we would start with drivers 2 and 3

To establish a **baseline for evaluating no-show rates among high-volume drivers**, we made a separate comparison using drivers with above-average trip counts. We defined **high volume by the number of recorded trips**, not by hours available, time spent driving, or fare revenue.

The 13 drivers with recorded trips averaged **15.38 trips each**, and eight exceeded that average. Together, those eight drivers recorded **seven rider no-shows across 145 trips**, producing a **baseline no-show rate of 4.83% for the high-volume group**.

This is different from the **25th-percentile cutoff** described earlier. The quartile cutoff screens out the smallest trip histories when comparing individual rates. The above-average trip-count requirement identifies the high-volume group whose combined results provide the 4.83% baseline. **The two cutoffs serve different purposes and include different numbers of drivers.**

To interpret this baseline for **driver 2**, we applied the group's 4.83% no-show rate to the driver's 16 trips. **If those 16 trips had occurred at the no-show rate recorded across the high-volume group, they would correspond to approximately 0.77 no-shows, or fewer than one on average.** Instead, driver 2 recorded **three no-shows in 16 trips (18.75%)**. This difference makes those trips a reasonable starting point for further investigation.

The **0.77** is a mathematical comparison, not a prediction that exactly zero or one event should have occurred. Driver 2's own trips are also included in the group's 4.83% baseline, so it isn't an independent comparison or evidence that the driver caused the incidents.

**Driver 3** recorded **two no-shows in 13 trips (15.38%)**. Those specific events deserve a closer look, although the trip history is still too limited to establish a recurring pattern. Driver 3 meets the quartile-based cutoff but does not belong to the separate eight-driver high-volume group. **Driver 4** recorded **one no-show in six trips (16.67%)**. That percentage is slightly higher than driver 3's, but it comes from a single event and an even smaller trip history. We'd continue monitoring driver 4 rather than treating the percentage alone as evidence of a problem.

## Other clues leadership shouldn't ignore

Because the recorded failure is a **rider** no-show, we also checked whether the same riders appeared repeatedly. **Riders 1 and 21 each have two no-show trips involving two different drivers.** Together, that's **four of the platform's 12 no-shows (33.33%)**. This doesn't establish exactly what happened during those trips, but it shows why an investigation shouldn't focus only on drivers. **Rider support** should be involved in understanding the actual pickup experiences.

We also compared the recorded **surge multipliers**. There were **seven no-shows in 46 trips at 1.25 (15.22%)**, versus **three in 99 at 1.00 (3.03%)**, **none in 29 at 1.50**, and **two in 26 at 2.00 (7.69%)**. The 1.25 figure catches the eye, but the pattern isn't consistent across multipliers and rests on only 12 failures overall. We have **not** established that surge pricing caused no-shows. It is a lead to test with more records, not a reason to change pricing policy now.

**Driver ratings also show why no-show rates shouldn't be treated as an overall measure of driver performance.** Drivers **2, 3, and 4** have recorded ratings of **4.94, 4.91, and 4.88**, respectively. All three are **among the five highest-rated drivers with recorded trips**, despite having some of the highest recorded no-show percentages. Meanwhile, the two lowest-rated drivers with trips, both rated **3.65**, each have just one no-show. In this dataset, a higher no-show rate does not automatically correspond to a lower driver rating.

More broadly, **driver performance involves more than one metric**. Trip volume, no-show rates, recorded fare totals, average fares, and driver ratings each tell us something different. That's why our supporting queries include several of these measures rather than relying on no-show percentages alone. Actual time spent driving and hours available for work could add further context, but our current analysis doesn't measure those factors. A driver with more trips or higher recorded fares isn't necessarily working more hours or providing better service.

The financial totals also need careful interpretation. **All 34 cancelled trips have $0 recorded fares**, while the platform's positive recorded fares sum to **$3,628.19** across completed and disputed trips. These are recorded amounts, not confirmation of collected payments or a calculation of money lost to no-shows.

## What we'd do next

**Driver operations and rider support** should jointly examine the individual no-show trips associated with **drivers 2 and 3**, looking for pickup-arrival confirmations, waiting times, communications, and relevant support records. Those details could help establish whether the incidents involved riders failing to arrive, difficulty locating the pickup point, communication problems, or circumstances that the trip summaries don't capture.

**Regional operations leaders** should review the pickup circumstances in the **North**, particularly Haiphong, and in **Hue** within Central. Because drivers operate outside their home locations, the relevant driver-management and pickup-region teams should coordinate these reviews rather than assuming the driver's home city is where the issue occurred. These locations are priorities for examining individual trips, not proven trouble spots.

**Driver 4** should remain under observation as more trips are recorded. One no-show in six trips is too little history to determine whether the percentage reflects a recurring pattern.

**Trust and Data** should help the investigating teams connect the relevant trip records with any available pickup, communication, and support information. The current data identifies which trips were marked as rider no-shows, but it doesn't tell us whether the driver reached the pickup point, what communications took place, or why a rider didn't appear. Obtaining that missing context would help leadership understand the incidents rather than relying only on summary percentages.

The repeat-rider and surge findings also deserve follow-up using a larger trip history. They offer useful questions for further investigation, but the current data is too limited to support conclusions about recurring rider behavior or the effects of surge pricing.

**The purpose of this analysis is to help leadership decide which incidents deserve closer investigation, how to prioritize that work, and what additional evidence is needed to understand what happened.**
