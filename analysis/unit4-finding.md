# Unit 4 Finding: The Real No-Show Rate—and Where Leadership Should Look Next

**To:** Leadership team  
**From:** Trust and Data team

**Bottom line:** The platform's actual recorded rider no-show rate is **6.0%—12 out of 200 trips**, not the roughly half shown on the old dashboard. We recommend examining the trips associated with **drivers 2 and 3** first. **Driver 4** also has a high percentage, but it comes from only one no-show in six trips, so we'd monitor rather than draw conclusions. A rider no-show is *associated with* a driver in our records; it does **not** establish that the driver caused it.

## What the corrected number includes

The 200 trip records contain **156 completed trips**, **34 cancellations**, and **10 disputes**. Only **12** meet the failure definition carried forward from our Unit 3 audit: a cancellation marked **“Rider no show.”** Counting all cancellations or disputes as this type of failure would misrepresent what we're investigating.

There are **15 registered drivers**, but **13 have recorded trips**. Among those 13, **four have no recorded rider no-shows**, **seven have one**, **driver 3 has two**, and **driver 2 has three**. Most no-shows are scattered across the platform; the following are the cases that most warrant context:

| Driver | No-show trips | All trips | Recorded rate | Recommendation |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| **2** | **3** | **16** | **18.75%** | Review first |
| **3** | **2** | **13** | **15.38%** | Review next |
| **4** | **1** | **6** | **16.67%** | Monitor; very small history |

Together, these three drivers are associated with **six of the 12 no-shows**, on **35 of the 200 trips**. Driver 4 stays in this table because the percentage is worth explaining, not because it's strong enough evidence for action. Our volume screen for a more cautious driver comparison is **13 trips**: drivers 2 and 3 meet it, while driver 4 does not. That screen reduces the influence of the smallest samples; it doesn't prove that everyone above it has a reliable long-term rate.

## Where no-shows were recorded

The **North** recorded **six no-shows in 77 trips (7.79%)**, **Central** recorded **five in 83 (6.02%)**, and the **South** recorded **one in 40 (2.50%)**. The whole-platform figure of **6.00%** lets leadership compare those areas with the same reference point. North is where we'd begin a region-level review, but a few events can shift any of these percentages significantly.

City detail shows why regional totals aren't enough:

| Recorded pickup city | No-show trips | All trips | Recorded rate |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Hue | 4 | 40 | 10.00% |
| Hanoi | 3 | 38 | 7.89% |
| Haiphong | 3 | 39 | 7.69% |
| Saigon | 1 | 40 | 2.50% |
| Danang | 1 | 43 | 2.33% |

**Hue and Danang are both included in Central**, yet Hue has four recorded no-shows and Danang one. The regional average hides that difference. It doesn't mean Hue has a proven ongoing problem; it tells the regional team which trips may deserve closer attention.

We also need to be clear about **whose region** we're discussing. These figures use **where the trip was supposed to start**, not where the driver lives. Only **67 of 200 trips** began in the driver's home region. A director responsible for service in a location may need the pickup view, while a leader managing drivers based in a location may need the home-region view. **Driver 2**, for example, is based in Danang, but their no-shows were recorded on pickups in **Hue and Haiphong**. The two ways of organizing the data answer different management questions.

## Why we would start with drivers 2 and 3

We compared drivers with **more trips than the average of 15.38** among drivers who have trip histories. Eight drivers qualified; together, their record is **seven no-shows in 145 trips**, or **4.83%**. That's a useful comparison group based on recorded trip count—not a claim that these drivers worked more hours, and not an independent prediction of what should happen.

At that **4.83%** group rate, **16 trips would correspond to about 0.77 no-show events**. Driver 2 had **three in 16 trips**, with an actual recorded rate of **18.75%**. Put simply, driver 2's record stands out even when we compare it with drivers who handled a similar or greater number of trips. The **0.77 is just a way of expressing the group rate across 16 trips**; it is not a predicted event count or evidence of responsibility. Driver 2's own trips are included in the group calculation.

Driver 3 has **two no-shows in 13 trips (15.38%)**, enough to warrant reviewing those specific events but not enough to reach conclusions about why they happened. Driver 3 meets the 13-trip minimum-volume screen, although they don't belong to the separate above-average-trip-count group. Driver 4's **one event in six trips** offers even less evidence of a recurring pattern. **No driver should be penalized from these percentages alone.**

## Other clues leadership shouldn't ignore

Because the recorded failure is a **rider** no-show, we also checked whether the same riders appeared repeatedly. **Riders 1 and 21 each have two no-show trips involving two different drivers.** Together, that's **four of the platform's 12 no-shows**. This doesn't prove those riders caused the records, but it does argue against looking only at drivers. **Rider support** should be involved in understanding the actual pickup experiences.

We also compared the recorded **surge multipliers**. There were **seven no-shows in 46 trips at 1.25 (15.22%)**, versus **three in 99 at 1.00 (3.03%)**, **none in 29 at 1.50**, and **two in 26 at 2.00**. The 1.25 figure catches the eye, but the pattern isn't consistent across multipliers and rests on only 12 failures overall. We have **not** established that surge pricing caused no-shows. It is a lead to test with more records, not a reason to change pricing policy now.

Driver ratings don't give us a simple answer either. Drivers **2, 3, and 4** have recorded ratings of **4.94, 4.91, and 4.88**; the two lowest-rated drivers **with trips** each have just one no-show. And the financial totals need careful interpretation: **all 34 cancelled trips have $0 recorded fares**, while the platform's positive recorded fares sum to **$3,628.19** across completed and disputed trips. These are recorded amounts, not confirmation of collected payments or a calculation of money lost to no-shows.

## What we'd do next

**Driver operations and rider support** should jointly examine the individual no-show trips associated with **drivers 2 and 3**, looking for pickup-arrival confirmations, waiting time, communications, and any relevant support records. **Regional operations leaders** should review the pickup circumstances in the **North**, and in **Hue** within Central, without treating those locations as proven trouble spots. Keep **driver 4** under observation as more trips accumulate.

**Trust and Data** should keep the failure definition fixed and check that future reporting uses the full trip denominator. **Payout leadership** should confirm whether a recorded rider no-show is even meant to count against a driver under the bonus rules before taking any action. The repeat-rider and surge findings deserve follow-up on a larger history, not immediate sanctions or pricing changes.

**The decision from this analysis is where to investigate—not whom to blame.**
